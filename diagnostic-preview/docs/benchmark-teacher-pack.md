# Benchmark teacher pack: original productive tasks and draft rating procedure

Version 1.0 · Review material for adults and students aged 16+ · **Not a validated examination or a CEFR placement instrument.**

This pack supplies writing and speaking evidence that a multiple-choice sample cannot supply. A1–C1 are intended task targets, not the levels automatically awarded for completing them. The tasks, rating descriptions and example responses are original. Descriptor references below are intended links to the [Council of Europe CEFR Companion Volume](https://rm.coe.int/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages-learning-teaching/16809ea0d4), expressed in our own words. This pack has not undergone formal standard setting, rater calibration or learner trials.

The online benchmark contains only thirty responses, including ten listening scripts awaiting approved human recordings. Its completion must not determine the starting task, a CEFR label or a course group through a raw percentage. Public benchmark tasks and their worked responses belong to practice and review material; do not later treat them as unseen secure assessment tasks.

## Administration and evidence rules

- Explain the purpose and conditions in the learner’s preferred interface language. English task prompts remain English; if an instruction is misunderstood, use an agreed plain-language clarification that changes neither the content nor the expected response. Record what was clarified. Do not translate the answer content or supply vocabulary during an assessed response.
- Begin with a brief unscored conversation and the learner’s relevant learning history. Select a manageable starting task from this pack. The target printed on a task is the author’s hypothesis; it is not a judgement about the learner. Use a fresh adjacent task when a boundary remains uncertain.
- Use two different writing functions and multiple speaking functions. Do not make somebody complete all five levels. Avoid interpreting a refusal to discuss personal circumstances as weak English: offer the listed fictional alternative.
- Agree and record use of dictionaries, spelling tools, AI, outside help, preparation time and accommodations. For an initial supervised reference sample, use no AI, translator or external assistance; do not silently compare this condition with an assisted home submission. Technical problems or an inaccessible task produce a rescheduled or alternative attempt, not a language penalty.
- Proposed writing slots are 12–18 minutes for the A1 pair, 18–25 for A2, 25–35 for B1 and 35–45 for B2/C1. These are pilot administration budgets, not CEFR standards. Word ranges below guide task scope, not automatic score deductions. Record time and completion; examine whether the limits distort the evidence.
- Plan approximately 18–22 minutes for a full speaking session, including a warm-up and, where needed, a fresh adjacent follow-up. A beginner may need a shorter productive segment. There is no requirement to fill time with inaccessible tasks. Preparation limits, prompt order and permitted examiner support must be fixed before comparing candidates.
- With informed permission, record speaking for marking and moderation. Recordings stay subject to the project’s consent, retention and access policy; permission to assess does not by itself permit marketing use, training use or public publication.
- The first productive-skill ratings are made without seeing online scores, course-sales information or a desired course group. Keep each rater’s original evidence and judgement before discussion. Double-mark all pilot work and operational boundary cases; investigate disagreements rather than averaging them away.

For each response record: candidate/session pseudonym; task and prompt version; actual time; relevant conditions and support; verbatim evidence or audio timestamps; what the evidence supports; what it cannot establish; first rater’s interpretation; second rater’s independent interpretation; disagreement; adjudication; any fresh task needed. A blank or too-short response is **insufficient evidence**, not automatically below A1.

## Original writing tasks

The shorter task tests an everyday communicative purpose. The second gives a different opportunity for connected or sustained production appropriate to the starting point. Neither task asks for specialist knowledge.

### Intended A1: a personal message and a simple description

**A1-W1 — A new class.**

> You are joining a weekend art class. Write a short message to the teacher. Say your name. Say which day you can come: Saturday or Sunday. Ask what time the class starts. You may use invented information.

Indicative scope: 20–40 words. Intended evidence: familiar personal information, a simple availability statement and a basic question. Scales: written interaction/correspondence and general linguistic range. Limit: successful use of a memorised message frame does not establish flexible sentence construction.

**A1-W2 — A familiar place.**

> Write about a room you know, or an invented room. Say what is in the room. Say one thing you do there and one thing you like about it.

Indicative scope: 25–45 words. Intended evidence: separate simple phrases or sentences about concrete surroundings and routine activities. Scale: overall written production/creative writing. Limit: do not require sophisticated linking or penalise the lack of an essay structure.

### Intended A2: a practical change and a short account

**A2-W1 — Change a collection time.**

> You arranged to collect a repaired bicycle at 4 p.m. on Friday. You now have to work until 5 p.m. Write to the repair shop. Explain the problem. Ask if you can collect it later on Friday or on Saturday. Ask what time the shop closes on Saturday.

Indicative scope: 45–70 words. Intended evidence: a clear routine request with simple connected information. Scales: correspondence/notes, messages and forms. Limit: there is no need to know repair-shop procedures; all necessary circumstances are provided.

**A2-W2 — A useful new habit.**

> Write about a small habit you started, or invent one. What do you do? When do you do it? Why did you start? Say one thing that is easy and one thing that is difficult.

Indicative scope: 55–85 words. Intended evidence: a sequence of simple linked statements about a familiar experience. Scale: creative writing. Limit: a habit’s health, moral or social value is not assessed.

### Intended B1: solving a problem and explaining an experience

**B1-W1 — An activity at a difficult time.**

> You booked a place on a two-session photography workshop. The organiser has moved the second session from Saturday morning to Friday afternoon. You work on Friday afternoons. Write an email explaining the problem. Suggest a practical alternative and ask one question that would help you decide what to do.

Indicative scope: 90–130 words. Intended evidence: connected explanation, a usable request and a relevant question. Scales: correspondence and goal-oriented written interaction. Limit: assess whether the alternative is understandable and relevant, not whether a real organiser would accept it.

**B1-W2 — A plan that changed.**

> Describe a time when a plan changed, or invent a realistic situation. Explain the original plan, what changed, what you did and what you learned or would do differently next time.

Indicative scope: 120–170 words. Intended evidence: a connected chronological account with reasons and a conclusion. Scale: creative writing. Limit: an ordinary incident is sufficient; personal drama or originality is not rewarded.

### Intended B2: negotiating a solution and weighing alternatives

**B2-W1 — Keeping a shared activity accessible.**

> You help run a weekly discussion club. The organiser proposes moving every meeting online because fewer people can attend in person. Some members have unreliable internet access. Write to the organiser. Acknowledge one benefit of the proposal, explain a concern, and recommend an arrangement that addresses both. Ask for the information you need before finalising it.

Indicative scope: 140–190 words. Intended evidence: appropriate register, clear qualification, developed practical reasoning and a specific request. Scales: correspondence/propositional precision. Limit: expertise in online meeting software is irrelevant.

**B2-W2 — Choosing a small community project.**

> A neighbourhood group can fund one project this year: a sheltered reading area in its garden or a monthly series of free practical workshops. Both cost the same. Write a recommendation for the group. Compare the likely benefits and limitations, choose an option, and explain what information might make you reconsider.

Indicative scope: 200–260 words. Intended evidence: a clear developed argument that weighs alternatives and recognises a relevant limitation. Scale: reports and essays. Limit: candidates may make reasonable assumptions if they identify them; no local policy or specialist financial knowledge is expected.

### Intended C1: sensitive reformulation and sustained qualified argument

**C1-W1 — A fair account of a disagreement.**

> You edit a community newsletter. A contributor calls a new pedestrian area “an obvious success” because cafés are busier. A shopkeeper says deliveries now take longer, and a resident says the quieter street matters more than business income. Write to the contributor. Explain why the current description needs revision, suggest how to preserve the supported positive claim while representing the disagreement fairly, and request one additional piece of evidence. Be direct without dismissing the contributor’s work.

Indicative scope: 180–230 words. Intended evidence: tactful precision, control of qualification and a coherent explanation of a sensitive revision. Scales: correspondence/propositional precision/sociolinguistic appropriateness. Limit: there is no single preferred political view or policy solution.

**C1-W2 — When a useful measure becomes the goal.**

> A community learning centre judges an event mainly by attendance. This makes its annual report easy to compare, but a small event may give participants more chances to practise and ask questions. Write an article for the centre’s members considering how success should be judged. Develop a position, examine the strongest objection to it, and propose a feasible way of reporting success without pretending that one measure captures everything. You may use the example above or another ordinary activity.

Indicative scope: 260–340 words. Intended evidence: sustained, well-structured treatment of a complex issue, integration of qualification and counterargument, and a purposeful conclusion. Scale: reports and essays. Limit: assess language and argument communication, not specialist statistical knowledge or agreement with a preferred management approach.

## Original speaking sets

Use the warm-up only to establish comfort and an appropriate starting point. In the assessed segment, retain a consistent sequence: familiar account, practical interaction, then follow-up. Examiner prompts must invite language rather than secretly test background knowledge. A learner can ask for a prompt to be repeated; record the kind and extent of help. A clarification strategy may be positive evidence, while repeated examiner reformulation limits what can be concluded about independent comprehension.

| Intended target | Short account | Practical interaction | Follow-up / fresh evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | “Tell me about a place where you study or relax. You can invent a place.” Use neutral prompts: “What is there?” “What do you do there?” | Candidate joins a simple class. Examiner has two possible days and two start times. Candidate asks the day/time, says which option works, and gives a name. Explain the role before starting; do not give the questions to repeat. | “Tell me about one thing you do on Saturday.” A fresh familiar topic checks whether language transfers beyond the practised exchange. |
| A2 | “Tell me about a recent free day, or an invented day. Where did you go? What did you do?” | Two people plan a short visit. Candidate’s card: free Sunday afternoon, cannot spend much, prefers being outside. Examiner offers a park walk or a café visit, then adds that rain is expected. Reach a practical arrangement. | “Why does that plan suit you?” “What could you do if it rains?” Check simple reasons and a short response to changed information. |
| B1 | “Describe something you learned to do. What was difficult at first, and what helped?” The alternative is an invented learner with an ordinary hobby. | Candidate and examiner plan a five-minute introduction for new club members. Candidate wants to explain activities; examiner thinks newcomers mainly need to meet people. Agree a plan and division of work. | “What might go wrong with the plan?” “How would you change it?” Check connected explanation and clarification, not a prepared speech alone. |
| B2 | “Describe a change that made a familiar activity better or worse. Explain why different people might see it differently.” | A club needs to reduce the work of organising events. Candidate proposes fewer large events; examiner favours small frequent meetings because newcomers find large groups difficult. Compare consequences, identify a trade-off and agree what to trial. | Examiner introduces a relevant complication: “Half the organisers can help only once a month.” Ask the candidate to reconsider and specify how the trial should be evaluated. |
| C1 | “Consider a case where a clear public message may leave out a real disagreement. How can someone be clear without giving a misleading impression?” Candidate may choose a familiar or invented example. | Candidate and examiner coordinate a volunteer cooking class advertised as hands-on. An experienced volunteer gives useful demonstrations, but these take nearly the whole session, leaving beginners little time to practise. Draft private feedback that acknowledges the useful expertise, identifies the problem and requests a specific change. The examiner proposes “You talk too much” and worries that polite language will hide the request. Negotiate wording that is direct and respectful. | “Could starting with praise make the requested change too easy to miss?” Then: “The volunteer replies, ‘So you don’t want me to explain anything?’ Respond without withdrawing the request or accepting that interpretation.” |

These are prompt sets, not scripts the examiner should force every learner through. Use a defined accessible alternative if the topic is unfamiliar. If a low target is clearly too easy, move to a fresh task with more demanding functions. If a task is inaccessible, obtain a successful performance at a lower demand instead of repeatedly eliciting failure. Record the route and support so that a later rater understands the evidence.

### Speaking interaction card details

For A1, prepare a simple examiner card with Saturday 10:00 and Sunday 14:00; the candidate may choose either. The score is not whether they choose a particular slot. For A2, the examiner first offers a free park walk and a café that requires buying a drink; introduce the forecast only after the candidate has expressed a preference. For B1–C1, the examiner must respond to what the learner actually says. Repeating a fixed question regardless of the learner’s answer would remove interaction from the construct.

For C1, give both participants the same facts: the demonstrations are useful, they take nearly the whole session, and the advertised format includes hands-on practice. The candidate does not need cooking expertise or a theory of teaching. The examiner’s blunt wording is a proposal to consider, not the required answer. Judge whether the learner can explain the effect of wording, preserve a clear request while acknowledging a contribution, and respond to the volunteer’s misinterpretation. Do not reward agreement with the examiner over a well-supported alternative. This scenario is separate from the online survey item; do not substitute that already seen numerical example for fresh productive evidence.

## Draft analytic rating framework

Do not add dimension scores and convert the total to CEFR. At this stage the rubric supports a documented qualitative judgement, followed by calibration work; it does not provide established cut scores. Report speaking and writing separately. A task target cannot be awarded simply because the candidate answered that task.

For each dimension record **supported features**, **limitations**, **evidence**, and **whether there was enough opportunity to observe it**. “Not observed” is different from “unable”. A conclusion should be supported across more than one relevant opportunity, with fresh evidence where the boundary matters.

| Writing dimension | What to observe | What not to substitute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communicative task fulfilment | Whether the reader can understand the relevant information, position, request or recommendation; development sufficient for the task. | Agreement with the teacher’s opinion; sophistication of life experience; a keyword checklist alone. |
| Organisation and cohesion | How ideas connect, progress and remain accessible; reference, paragraphing and cohesive choices appropriate to the task. | A mandatory five-paragraph template or counting linking words. |
| Range and precision | Available vocabulary and structures, flexibility of formulation, and the precision needed to express distinctions and qualifications. | Rare words used inaccurately or ambitious words rewarded merely for being rare. |
| Control | Grammatical and lexical control, spelling and punctuation, considered in relation to meaning and task demand. | Counting every surface error equally or penalising one variety’s legitimate usage. |
| Register and reader awareness | Language appropriate to relationship, purpose and reader; politeness and clarity in requests or disagreement. | A single preferred accent, dialect, cultural style or ceremonial level of formality. |

| Speaking dimension | What to observe | What not to substitute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communicative development | Relevant connected responses, explanations and support appropriate to the prompt. | Length of speech without useful meaning. |
| Interaction | Responding to the partner, taking and offering turns, negotiating, asking for clarification and repairing misunderstandings. | A memorised monologue or agreement with every examiner suggestion. |
| Range, precision and control | Resources available while speaking, accuracy in relation to demand, and whether distinctions are communicated. | A transcript-only judgement of the complete speaking skill. |
| Fluency and organisation | Continuity, meaningful grouping and management of pauses or reformulation, observed in the recording. | Speaking as fast as possible or treating all pauses as defects. |
| Phonological intelligibility | Whether sounds, stress, rhythm and intonation allow the intended meaning to be understood; effort needed by the listener. | Native-likeness, nationality, or unfamiliarity with a legitimate accent. |

### Working reference descriptions, not calibrated band anchors

These brief descriptions focus attention during review; they are not complete official CEFR descriptors and are not an operational rating scale. Replace broad impressions with observed evidence. “Consistent” below means within the actual sampled tasks, not every possible context.

| Candidate evidence near an intended target | Writing features to investigate | Speaking features to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | Familiar words and basic phrases convey simple personal information; separate simple sentences may require generous reader interpretation. | Very short familiar exchanges and concrete information are possible with a supportive interlocutor; substantial pauses and limited resources are compatible with the target. |
| A2 | Simple sentences linked with common connectors convey routine messages, descriptions and a short sequence; basic errors may remain. | Routine exchanges and short accounts are understandable; support may be needed to maintain the interaction beyond familiar material. |
| B1 | Connected straightforward writing communicates experiences, reasons and practical requests; errors do not regularly obscure the main message. | A connected account and explanation are possible on familiar matters; the speaker can respond, clarify and manage ordinary practical problems despite hesitation. |
| B2 | Clear developed writing weighs alternatives or supports a position, with relevant qualification and generally effective organisation and control. | Clear sustained contributions and active interaction allow discussion of alternatives and implications; occasional searching does not regularly disrupt meaning. |
| C1 | Well-structured extended writing handles complexity, qualification and reader needs with flexible, precise formulation and high control. | Flexible, well-organised speech can express fine distinctions, follow and reshape an argument, and interact effectively without much obvious restriction of what the speaker intends to say. |

Sources for intended orientation: CEFR scales for overall written production; creative writing; reports and essays; correspondence; overall oral production; sustained monologue (experience, information and argument); overall oral interaction; informal discussion; goal-oriented co-operation; general linguistic range; vocabulary range/control; grammatical accuracy; fluency; coherence and cohesion; propositional precision; phonological control. A level interpretation still requires the project’s standard-setting and validation process.

## Two original writing exemplars for reviewer discussion

These responses are fictional. They are deliberately not labelled as validated band exemplars. They show how feedback can cite evidence without pretending that a few displayed sentences establish a level.

### Exemplar 1: response to B1-W1

> Hello,
>
> I booked your photography workshop last week. I was happy with the Saturday times because I work in a shop on Fridays. Now the second meeting is on Friday afternoon and I cannot leave my work early.
>
> Could I join another group for the second meeting? If there is no other group, perhaps I can do a task at home and send you my pictures. I know this is not same as being in the class, but I still want to learn.
>
> Will the Friday meeting include the outdoor activity? That is the part I most want to do.
>
> Thank you for your help.
>
> Arman

Evidence to discuss:

- The time conflict and requested action are clear. The alternative is relevant, and the final question helps the writer decide. These support communicative fulfilment for this task.
- “If there is no other group” connects a contingency to a second proposal. “I know this is not same…” recognises a limitation rather than simply repeating a demand.
- “Not same” needs “not **the** same”; this local error does not conceal the request. The visible success does not establish control of a wide grammar range.
- A second task is needed to observe connected narration and whether the apparent strengths transfer. The excerpt alone does not establish B1, a sublevel, or readiness for a specific class.

### Exemplar 2: response to B2-W1

> Dear Jo,
>
> Moving the discussion club online would certainly make it easier for members who travel a long way or finish work late. I would be concerned, though, if it became the only way to attend. Two members have already mentioned that their connections often fail, so the change could solve one access problem while creating another.
>
> Could we try alternating online and in-person meetings for a month? That would not give everyone equal access every week, but it would preserve a regular opportunity to participate. We could send the discussion questions in advance and share a short summary afterwards, although these should support attendance rather than be treated as an equivalent experience.
>
> Before confirming a trial, could you check how many members would use each format and whether someone can host the online sessions? At the end of the month, I suggest asking about missed contributions as well as attendance. A full screen of names would not necessarily tell us who was able to join the discussion.
>
> Best,
>
> Leila

Evidence to discuss:

- The response acknowledges a benefit, identifies a concrete concern and develops a feasible proposal. It does not merely name a trade-off.
- “Would not give everyone equal access every week” and “support attendance rather than…” qualify the recommendation. They offer evidence of precise scope in this task.
- The final evaluation criterion develops the access concern across the message. Clear paragraphing supports the reader’s decision.
- This strong response at the intended task demand is not automatically “C1” because it contains qualifications, and not automatically “B2” because the prompt was labelled B2. A rating needs the full performance set, independent judgement and calibrated reference performances. There may be too little error evidence here to identify the writer’s limits.

## Speaking annotation demonstration

Written transcript fragments can show semantic choices and interactional moves. They cannot establish actual fluency, pause timing, pronunciation, prosody or how much help the real interlocutor supplied.

**Fictional B1-task fragment:**

> Examiner: We may not have enough time for both introductions and a long explanation.
>
> Candidate: When you say introductions, do you mean everyone says their name, or that they talk in pairs?
>
> Examiner: I mean a short activity in pairs.
>
> Candidate: Then I would keep that. People can learn the activities from a paper, but they need a chance to meet somebody. We could explain only how to join the next meeting.

Potential evidence: a specific clarification request; a relevant response to the clarification; a reason that distinguishes two functions; a workable compromise. Not established from this text: spoken fluency, intonation, spontaneous delivery or CEFR classification.

**Fictional C1-task fragment:**

> Examiner: If you start by praising the demonstrations, won’t the volunteer think everything is fine?
>
> Candidate: They might, if the request is left as a hint. I would make it a separate sentence: “Could we keep the opening demonstration to fifteen minutes, then let people try while you help them?” That asks for a different use of the expertise, not less of it.
>
> Examiner: The volunteer replies, “So you don’t want me to explain anything?”
>
> Candidate: I would say, “Your explanations are useful. The change is when people receive them. We need to protect time for practice, and you could explain the next step while they are trying it.” I wouldn’t withdraw the time limit just to avoid the disagreement.

Potential evidence: engagement with an objection about indirectness, a concrete request, a distinction between rejecting expertise and changing its use, and repair of a misinterpretation without surrendering the original purpose. The proposed fifteen-minute limit is an invented practical suggestion, not a supplied fact or the only acceptable solution. Not established: a general C1 speaking level. A real spontaneous recording, other tasks and independent ratings are required.

## Decision and reporting rules

1. Summarise the supported writing and speaking features separately, with actual excerpts or audio timestamps. Name only strengths and priorities that the evidence supports; do not fill a quota.
2. Where the evidence leaves an adjacent boundary unresolved, state the specific missing demand and obtain a fresh task. For example, clear routine explanations may be secure while sustained weighing of alternatives remains unobserved. Do not replace that explanation with a broad cross-skill range.
3. If the learner’s apparent difficulty could come from the task, instructions, anxiety, conditions or recording, investigate and resample before interpreting it as a language limit.
4. A skill label becomes operational only through the broader project’s standard setting and held-out validation. This benchmark may generate informative observations and a personal practice suggestion, but it cannot justify paid claims of an exact level, beginning/middle/end sublevels, or IELTS equivalence.
5. Decide course fit separately after checking access to the actual class’s spoken instructions, task demands, available support and workload. A strong reading result cannot erase a listening barrier. A course sale must not alter the reported evidence.

## Review and pilot gates for this pack

- Independent task reviewers attempt each prompt and identify plausible misunderstandings, inaccessible assumptions, hidden background knowledge and unequal opportunity to demonstrate the intended construct.
- Review RU/EN administration instructions for equivalent support. Any translation of a task prompt must be versioned and trialled before it is treated as equivalent.
- Trial timing and prompt accessibility with actual learners across the intended range, including those who decline personal disclosure or need an accommodation. Record incomplete tasks and reasons.
- Assemble actual learner performances for calibration, with independent judgements before consensus. The two fictional exemplars above cannot serve as an external standard or replace real benchmark performances.
- Double-mark pilot performances and examine agreement, direction and causes of disagreement by task and skill, including boundary and uneven-profile cases. Preserve initial ratings; do not report consensus agreement as independent inter-rater reliability.
- Establish and document the reference decision process and marking conditions before applying the numerical validation gates in the rebuild plan. No numerical target in that plan is evidence that this pack has already met it.
