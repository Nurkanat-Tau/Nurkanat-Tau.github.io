# Assessment v2: review exemplars and production acceptance

**Status:** original review material for an audience aged 16+. These examples are not calibrated, are not in the scored bank, and have no production audio. A1–C1 labels below are intended authoring targets for review, not validated classifications.

The candidate inventory contains **600 online items: 200 language-use, 200 reading, and 200 listening**, with 40 items per domain per intended level. This is a candidate floor for the planned 120-item core design, subject to form-assembly, retake, and extension feasibility. The exemplars do not settle routing, score interpretation, or administration time.

Listening production uses approximately **80 human-recorded stimuli**: A1 20, A2 16, B1 16, B2 14, C1 14. Two linked items per A1 stimulus yield 40; a mixture of two and three yields 40 at each other level. Linked questions remain a stimulus cluster in analysis, not independent confirmations of ability. Approve **10 proof recordings, two per level**, before full production; cast **6–8 authentic voices** across the bank.

## Five illustrative tasks

### 1. A1 target: practical reading

**Input — study-room notice**

> STUDY ROOM  
> Open Monday–Friday, 9:00–17:00.  
> Please book at the front desk before you enter.  
> Water is welcome. Please leave food and hot drinks outside.  
> Put your chair back when you leave.

**Question:** What can you take into the study room?

A. A cup of hot tea.  
B. A bottle of water.  
C. A sandwich.

**Key: B.** Water is explicitly permitted. A and C are realistic things a visitor might bring, but both are excluded by the notice. No rule about libraries or classrooms needs to be inferred.

**Construct:** finding one explicit practical permission in a short notice.  
**Excluded knowledge:** local venue policies, payment systems, specialist vocabulary.  
**Reviewer fields:** accessible instruction __; key unique __; target appropriate __; vocabulary burden __; decision/revision __.

### 2. A2 target: listening to changed arrangements

**Human-recording script; two friends, conversational and unhurried.**

**Nina:** Are you still coming on the photography walk on Saturday?  
**Omar:** Yes. Outside the library at ten, isn't it?  
**Nina:** Ten is right, but the library entrance is closed for repairs. We're meeting outside the café across the road instead.  
**Omar:** Oh, right. Do I need to pay before Saturday?  
**Nina:** No, you can pay when you arrive. But you need to book a place by Friday. There's a form on the club website.  
**Omar:** I'll do that tonight. Can I use my phone for the pictures?  
**Nina:** Of course. You don't need a camera.

**Q1. Where will they meet?** A. At the library entrance. B. Outside the café opposite the library. C. Inside the café.  
**Key: B.** A is the superseded arrangement; C changes the stated position.

**Q2. What does Omar need to do by Friday?** A. Pay for the walk. B. Get a camera. C. Reserve a place online.  
**Key: C.** Payment happens on arrival; a separate camera is unnecessary.

**Construct:** tracking an updated arrangement and distinguishing a required action from optional preparation.  
**Excluded knowledge:** photography, prices, website navigation. The form is described, not operated.  
**Recording direction:** do not stress the key words as an answer cue. Preserve the contrast between old and new arrangements naturally.  
**Reviewer fields:** heard key without transcript __; competing interpretation __; repair intelligible __; reading burden __; decision __.

### 3. B1 target: contextual language and time relationships

**Situation:** Dina reached the bus stop at 8:10. The bus departed at 8:05. She explains why she took a taxi.

**Question:** Which message accurately describes what happened?

A. When I reached the stop, the bus had already left.  
B. When I reached the stop, the bus was about to leave.  
C. When I reached the stop, the bus was still waiting.

**Key: A.** The departure precedes arrival. B describes an imminent departure and C a bus still present. All options are grammatical, so the task tests meaning rather than elimination of broken sentences.

**Construct:** recognising a temporal sequence in a practical explanation. The cues **already**, **about to**, and **still** can support the choice; success does not independently establish mastery or productive use of the past perfect.  
**Excluded knowledge:** transport rules, fares, arithmetic beyond ordering two times.  
**Reviewer fields:** meaning distinction clear __; unwanted numerical burden __; target too easy __; plausible distractors __; decision __.

### 4. B2 target: listening to negotiation and qualified agreement

**Human-recording script; two colleagues arranging an open day.**

**Lena:** The queue was nearly an hour last Saturday. If we cut each tour to twenty minutes, we could get everyone through much faster.  
**Ben:** On paper, yes. But the visitors kept saying how useful it was to ask the guide questions. I'd be reluctant to lose that.  
**Lena:** So we'd leave people standing outside?  
**Ben:** That's not quite what I mean. They hear the same introduction at the desk and again when the tour starts. Could we stop doing it twice?  
**Lena:** That might save five minutes. We could give them a simple map at the desk too, rather than explain the whole route there.  
**Ben:** Yes, as long as the guide still has time for questions.  
**Lena:** Let's try that for one Saturday. We'll check how long people wait and ask whether they felt rushed.  
**Ben:** That would tell us more than just counting how many people we got through.

**Q1. What change do they agree to trial?** A. Shorten the question period on every tour. B. Remove repeated introductory information and provide a map. C. Replace guided tours with independent visits.  
**Key: B.** This combines the proposal and agreement. A is precisely what Ben wants to preserve; C overextends the purpose of the map.

**Q2. What is Ben's position?** A. Shorter waiting times matter, but visitors should still get a useful visit. B. Visitors would prefer to wait rather than accept any change to the tour. C. The number of completed tours is the best way to judge the trial.  
**Key: A.** Ben supports a change subject to preserving value. B mistakes a reservation for opposition; C reverses his closing qualification.

**Construct:** following a proposal, challenge, clarification, and conditional agreement.  
**Excluded knowledge:** event management or queue calculations.  
**Recording direction:** the clarification is calm, not an argument. Record both performers together or in a live remote exchange; no isolated turn-by-turn recital.  
**Reviewer fields:** speaker distinction __; intent recoverable from language __; key not carried solely by theatrical delivery __; decision __.

### 5. C1 target: discourse and evaluative stance

**Original fictional review extract**

> By the final chapter of Mara's memoir, she and her sister have not reconciled. Several reviewers have treated this as a failure to provide an ending. To my mind, it is the book's most convincing choice. A neat reunion would require the sisters to agree on a version of their childhood that neither can honestly accept. The absence of agreement gives the earlier conversations their weight. My reservation lies elsewhere. Mara reminds us so frequently that her memory is unreliable that the admission begins to sound like a performance of modesty. The quieter passages, in which a small detail unsettles an earlier certainty, do the work more effectively. The book earns its unresolved ending; it does not always trust the reader enough to leave its uncertainty unannounced.

**Q1. Which judgement best represents the reviewer?** A. The unresolved ending is convincing, although the narrator sometimes overstates her uncertainty. B. The candid discussion of memory makes up for an unconvincing unresolved ending. C. The sisters' eventual agreement gives the memoir its strength despite its hesitant narration.  
**Key: A.** The praise concerns the ending; the reservation concerns presentation. B swaps these evaluations, while C invents a reunion.

**Q2. What does “a performance of modesty” imply here?** A. The admissions increasingly seem self-conscious rather than naturally revealing. B. The repeated qualifications provide convincing evidence of genuine impartiality. C. The narrator uses uncertainty mainly to excuse her sister's behaviour.  
**Key: A.** Repetition makes the disclaimer feel staged. B treats the modesty literally and misses the criticism; C supplies a motive the reviewer does not attribute to the narrator.

**Construct:** tracking the scope of praise and reservation and interpreting a figurative evaluation.  
**Excluded knowledge:** any real book, literary history, or family-reconciliation norms.  
**Reviewer fields:** evidence for each judgement __; cultural assumptions __; alternative reading __; lexical burden __; decision __.

## Illustrative completed report page

**Illustration only — fictional learner and evidence, not a result produced by the current site.**

### Your English works differently across skills

| Evidence area | Assessed finding | What this means in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading | B2 | Follows contrasting views and distinguishes the writer's main position from a reservation. |
| Listening | A2 | Understands clear practical details; loses information when speakers revise plans or imply disagreement. |
| Speaking | B1, teacher reviewed | Gives connected explanations; sometimes needs a follow-up question repeated or rephrased. |
| Writing | B1, teacher reviewed | Communicates the intended message and connects familiar ideas; tense control and reference need work. |
| Language use | B1 supporting evidence | Recognises several structures more reliably than using them in original speaking and writing. |

**Teacher placement:** B1 group with a focused listening bridge. This is a course decision, not an average or an “A2–B2 overall level.” Reading strength means a complete repeat of elementary reading work would be inefficient.

**Before confirming the group:** check that ordinary classroom explanations can be followed with occasional clarification. If routine translation or repeated simplification is necessary, use a short bridge and reassess placement. A B2 group additionally requires evidence of managing its listening, discussion, and writing demands; B2 reading alone does not meet those requirements.

**Evidence illustrated:** the learner identified a writer's qualified recommendation; missed a changed meeting arrangement; explained a personal choice coherently; and used two incompatible past-time forms in a written account. The real report must identify its actual tasks, excerpts, or speaking timestamps instead of copying these examples.

**Next four weeks:** practise short conversations for main point, then changed details; retell a recording in three sentences; revise two personal narratives with consistent time relationships. Keep reading at a challenging level. Review progress on unseen material, not the same assessment questions.

**Useful deliverable:** a skill profile, up to three evidenced priorities, selected writing corrections, speaking examples, a four-week plan, and a reasoned placement recommendation. No fabricated precision, guaranteed level gain, or filler pages.

## Recording audition and acceptance sheet

Audition each voice with an everyday arrangement and a tactful disagreement. Ask for natural interaction, then one directed retake. Capture performer ID, authentic variety, recording setup, permitted uses, director, takes, and decision. Confirm commercial usage and editing rights before commissioning; no assumed voice-cloning permission.
